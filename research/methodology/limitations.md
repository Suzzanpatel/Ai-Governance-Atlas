# AI Governance Atlas

## Research Limitations

### 1. Purpose

This document identifies the principal limitations affecting the design,
evidence base, computational analysis and interpretation of AI
Governance Atlas. Its purpose is to ensure that the project's
conclusions remain proportionate to the material examined and that
uncertainty is made explicit.

### 2. Scope and Sample Size

The current annotated evidence dataset contains 20 selected provisions:
five each for India, the European Union (EU), the United Kingdom (UK)
and the OECD AI Principles as an international benchmark.

The sample is purposive rather than random. It is designed to support a
focused comparison across five governance dimensions, but it is not
necessarily representative of the full body of AI-related law, policy or
institutional practice in any of the frameworks.

Consequently: - Findings describe the selected provisions, not every
instrument or institution in each framework. - Equal numbers of records
do not establish that the frameworks are substantively equivalent. - The
dataset cannot support statistical generalisation to an entire
jurisdiction or international organisation. - Additional provisions or
instruments may change the patterns observed in the current sample.

### 3. Differences in Legal Status and Instrument Type

The evidence pack includes different types of instruments: binding
legislation, non-binding guidance, government policy and international
recommendations.

These instruments do not have equivalent legal force, institutional
authority or enforcement consequences. A recommendation in an
international instrument cannot be treated as equivalent to a statutory
duty in legislation. Similarly, the presence of a policy principle does
not by itself establish a legally enforceable obligation.

The coding framework therefore separates: - Legal force of the
instrument. - Obligation force of the specific provision. - Nature of
the obligation. - Responsible actor. - Enforcement mechanism.

Despite these distinctions, cross-framework comparison remains
interpretive. Classifications must be grounded in the wording and legal
context of each primary source.

### 4. Differences in Institutional and Regulatory Context

India, the EU and the UK have different constitutional, institutional
and regulatory arrangements. The OECD is an international organisation
and its AI Principles operate as an international benchmark rather than
as a domestic legal system.

The same governance term may have different institutional meanings or
practical consequences across these settings. For example, oversight,
accountability or enforcement may be allocated to different actors or
operate through different legal mechanisms.

The project uses common coding categories to support comparison, but
these categories cannot remove the underlying differences between the
frameworks. Findings should explain relevant contextual differences
rather than assume that similarly labelled provisions perform identical
functions.

### 5. Document Selection, Length and Publication Date

The source documents may differ in length, structure, drafting style,
publication date, scope and level of detail. These differences can
affect both manual and computational comparison.

Longer documents may contain more occurrences of a keyword simply
because they contain more text. Differences in terminology may also
cause conceptually similar requirements to be expressed using different
words.

The project should record source titles, issuing bodies, dates,
provision references and official source locations. Where a document has
been amended or consolidated, the version used for coding should be
identified clearly.

### 6. Limitations of PDF Extraction and Text Preprocessing

PDF text extraction may introduce errors or omissions, particularly
where documents contain complex layouts, tables, footnotes, headers,
scanned pages or unusual formatting.

Preprocessing may also remove or alter information relevant to
interpretation, including punctuation, section headings,
cross-references or legal qualifiers.

Extracted text should therefore be treated as an analytical aid, not as
a substitute for the authoritative source document. Material legal
classifications must be checked against the original official text.

### 7. Limitations of Keyword Analysis and NLP

Keyword analysis and other natural language processing (NLP) techniques
can help identify recurring terms and patterns across a documentary
corpus. However, they have important limitations:

-   A keyword occurrence does not necessarily indicate a legal
    obligation.
-   The same term may have different meanings in different legal or
    policy contexts.
-   Similar concepts may be expressed using different terminology.
-   Automated methods may miss negation, qualifications, exceptions,
    cross-references and the relationship between provisions.
-   Frequency counts can be influenced by document length and drafting
    style.
-   Keyword analysis alone cannot determine whether an obligation is
    binding, enforceable or implemented.

Computational findings should therefore be interpreted as descriptive
indicators. They must be complemented by manual legal and policy
analysis.

### 8. Subjectivity in Manual Coding

Manual classification requires interpretation, particularly where a
provision serves more than one governance function or where the source
does not clearly specify the force of an obligation.

Reasonable reviewers may disagree about a provision's primary governance
dimension, obligation nature or responsible actor. To reduce
inconsistency, the project uses a written coding framework, records
coding rationales and preserves uncertainty where the source is
ambiguous.

The current structural audit checks matters such as duplicate
identifiers, missing values and category distributions. A structural
audit does not independently establish that every legal classification
is substantively correct. Source-level verification remains necessary.

### 9. Implementation Evidence Limitations

The existence or publication of a law, policy or recommendation does not
by itself demonstrate that it has been implemented in practice.

Implementation evidence may be incomplete, unavailable, inconsistent or
difficult to compare across frameworks. Public documents may not capture
internal regulatory activity, compliance practices or outcomes.

The project uses five implementation categories: Implemented, Partially
implemented, Not yet implemented, No evidence of implementation found
and Not assessed.

These categories must be applied cautiously: - "Not assessed" means no
implementation assessment has been made. - "No evidence of
implementation found" describes the result of a documented search; it
does not prove that implementation has not occurred. - "Partially
implemented" and "Implemented" require specific documentary support. -
The absence of located evidence must not be converted into a conclusion
of non-compliance.

Implementation claims should identify the evidence source, issuing
organisation, date and relevant passage or page.

### 10. Limits on Assessing Regulatory Effectiveness

This project examines selected documentary provisions and uses
computational and qualitative methods to compare their stated
requirements and mechanisms. It does not, on the basis of the current
20-record dataset alone, measure real-world compliance, reductions in
AI-related harms, institutional performance or causal regulatory impact.

The presence of a detailed legal obligation does not automatically
demonstrate effective enforcement or successful outcomes. Conversely,
the absence of a specific provision in the selected sample does not
establish that a framework lacks the relevant governance function
elsewhere.

Conclusions about regulatory effectiveness must therefore be framed as
limited assessments of the selected provisions and available evidence,
not definitive rankings of entire governance systems.

### 11. Comparability of the OECD Benchmark

The OECD AI Principles are used as an international benchmark. They are
not a fourth jurisdiction and should not be analysed as though they were
directly equivalent to domestic legislation or government policy.

Their role is to provide an international reference for principles and
recommendations. Comparisons involving the OECD should explicitly
account for its different institutional and legal status.

### 12. Reproducibility and Version Control

The results depend on the documents selected, the versions consulted,
the extraction and preprocessing methods, and the coding decisions
applied.

To improve reproducibility, the project should retain: - Original source
documents and official references. - The version and date of each source
used. - Extracted and processed text. - The annotated dataset and
coding-review template. - The coding framework and methodological
documents. - Analysis scripts and generated outputs. - A record of
material coding changes and their rationale.

Changes to source documents, sample selection or coding rules may affect
the results and should be documented.

### 13. Overall Interpretive Caution

AI Governance Atlas is a focused, exploratory comparative study. Its
contribution is to demonstrate a transparent method for examining
selected AI governance provisions through documentary analysis, manual
coding and computational text analysis.

The strength of its conclusions depends on the quality and traceability
of the primary sources, the consistency of the coding process and the
availability of implementation evidence. Findings should remain limited
to what the selected documents and verified evidence can support.
